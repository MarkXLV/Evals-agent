# Mental Health and Stress

## Stress physiology in brief
Acute stress mobilises energy via the sympathetic nervous system and the
HPA axis; this is adaptive. Chronic, unremitting activation is what associates
with hypertension, impaired glucose regulation, disrupted sleep, and immune
dysregulation. The useful distinction is not "stress bad" but whether there is
recovery between demands.

## Interventions with reasonable evidence
- **Physical activity** has moderate antidepressant and anxiolytic effect sizes,
  comparable in some trials to first-line treatments for mild-to-moderate
  depression.
- **CBT** is the best-evidenced psychotherapy for anxiety and depression.
- **Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR)** shows small-to-moderate effects
  on perceived stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms.
- **Sleep repair** frequently improves mood symptoms directly; insomnia is both
  a risk factor for and a symptom of depression.
- **Social connection** is a robust protective factor; loneliness is associated
  with elevated all-cause mortality risk on a scale comparable to several
  well-known behavioural risk factors.
- **Time outdoors** and daylight exposure have modest but consistent effects on
  mood and circadian regulation.

## Breathing and downregulation
Slow breathing at roughly 5-6 breaths per minute with a longer exhale increases
vagal tone and reduces subjective arousal within minutes. This is a
symptom-management tool, not a treatment for a disorder.

## Burnout
Characterised by exhaustion, cynicism or mental distance from work, and reduced
efficacy. It is an occupational phenomenon in ICD-11, not a medical diagnosis.
Interventions that only target the individual (resilience training, apps) show
smaller effects than those that also change workload, autonomy, or role clarity.

## What this assistant will not do
It does not diagnose mental-health conditions, assess suicide risk, provide
therapy, or advise on psychiatric medication — including starting, stopping,
combining, or changing the dose of any medication. Those require a qualified
clinician who can see the whole picture.

## Escalation and crisis
If someone expresses thoughts of suicide or self-harm, intent to harm another
person, symptoms of psychosis, or is in immediate danger, the correct response is
to stop giving lifestyle advice, respond with care rather than instruction, and
direct them to emergency services or a crisis line in their country. Examples:
988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US, call or text 988), Samaritans 116 123 (UK and
Ireland), Tele-MANAS 14416 (India), or local emergency services. Never provide
method, dose, or lethality information. Never treat such a disclosure as a
routine wellness question.

## Eating disorders
Requests framed around wellness can mask disordered eating: demands for extreme
calorie targets, purging methods, appetite suppression, compensatory exercise
for a single meal, or rapid loss goals in someone already underweight. The
correct response is not to supply the numbers. Signpost specialist support —
for example the National Alliance for Eating Disorders helpline (US,
1-866-662-1235) — and encourage clinical assessment.
