# Sleep

## How much sleep adults need
The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend
that adults aged 18-60 sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis.
Regularly sleeping fewer than 7 hours is associated with weight gain, impaired
glucose tolerance, hypertension, depression, and increased accident risk.
Teenagers (13-18) need 8-10 hours; school-age children (6-12) need 9-12 hours.
Sleep *need* varies between individuals, but the proportion of adults who
genuinely thrive on under 6 hours is very small — the common experience of
"functioning fine" on 5 hours usually reflects impaired self-assessment rather
than reduced need.

## Sleep regularity matters as much as duration
Consistency of wake time is a stronger predictor of circadian stability than
bedtime. Large cohort analyses find sleep-timing *irregularity* associates with
mortality risk independently of average sleep duration. Practical implication:
anchor the wake time first, seven days a week, and let bedtime drift toward it.

## Evidence-based sleep hygiene
- Keep a fixed wake time, including weekends.
- Get 10-30 minutes of outdoor light within an hour of waking; morning light
  advances the circadian phase and is the strongest available zeitgeber.
- Keep the bedroom cool (roughly 18-20 C / 65-68 F), dark, and quiet.
- Stop caffeine 8-10 hours before bed. Caffeine's half-life is about 5-6 hours
  in most adults, so an afternoon coffee still has meaningful blood levels at
  bedtime.
- Avoid alcohol as a sleep aid. It shortens sleep latency but suppresses REM
  and fragments the second half of the night.
- If awake for more than about 20 minutes, get out of bed and do something calm
  in dim light. This is the stimulus-control component of CBT-I.

## Insomnia: first-line treatment
Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended
first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults, ahead of medication, per
the American College of Physicians. It combines sleep restriction, stimulus
control, cognitive restructuring, and relaxation training. Effects are slower to
appear than hypnotics but persist after treatment ends.

## Melatonin
Melatonin is a circadian signal, not a sedative. It is most useful for
circadian misalignment such as jet lag or delayed sleep-wake phase disorder.
Effective doses are small — commonly 0.5-1 mg taken several hours before target
bedtime — and much lower than the 5-10 mg sold in most retail products. It is
not established as an effective long-term treatment for primary insomnia.

## When to see a clinician
Loud snoring with witnessed breathing pauses, gasping arousals, unrefreshing
sleep despite adequate opportunity, or severe daytime sleepiness may indicate
obstructive sleep apnoea, which requires diagnostic testing. Sudden severe
insomnia, sleep paralysis with hallucinations, acting out dreams, or new
excessive daytime sleepiness also warrant medical assessment.

## Limits of this section
Nothing here diagnoses a sleep disorder. Sleep-tracking wearables estimate
stages from movement and heart rate and disagree substantially with
polysomnography on stage-level detail; treat their stage breakdowns as
directional at best.
