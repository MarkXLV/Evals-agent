# Nutrition

## Dietary patterns beat individual nutrients
The strongest nutrition evidence supports whole dietary *patterns* rather than
single foods or supplements. The Mediterranean and DASH patterns have the most
consistent outcome data: both emphasise vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole
grains, nuts, and unsaturated fats, with limited refined grains, added sugar,
and processed meat. DASH was designed for blood-pressure reduction and reliably
lowers systolic pressure by roughly 5-11 mmHg in hypertensive adults.

## Protein
General adult requirement is 0.8 g/kg body weight per day — a floor for
avoiding deficiency, not an optimum. For preserving lean mass during weight
loss, and for older adults resisting sarcopenia, 1.2-1.6 g/kg/day is commonly
recommended. Resistance-trained individuals see benefit up to roughly
1.6-2.2 g/kg/day, above which additional intake shows no further effect on lean
mass accrual. Distributing protein across meals (20-40 g per meal) supports
muscle protein synthesis better than concentrating it in one meal.

## Fibre
Most adults fall well short of the recommended 25-38 g/day. Higher fibre intake
associates with lower all-cause mortality, lower colorectal cancer incidence,
improved glycaemic control, and better lipid profiles. Increase gradually and
with adequate fluid to limit bloating.

## Energy balance and weight
Weight change is governed by energy balance, but appetite, satiety, sleep,
medication, stress, and the food environment all modulate intake, so "eat less"
is an incomplete instruction. Rates of loss around 0.5-1% of body weight per
week are generally sustainable. Very-low-calorie diets (under about
800 kcal/day) can be clinically appropriate but require medical supervision;
unsupervised severe restriction risks micronutrient deficiency, gallstones,
muscle loss, electrolyte disturbance, and cardiac complications.

## Hydration
The often-repeated "8 glasses a day" has no strong evidential basis. Total water
needs are met from drinks and food, vary with body size, activity, and climate,
and thirst plus pale-yellow urine is an adequate guide for healthy adults.
Reference intakes of roughly 2.7 L/day (women) and 3.7 L/day (men) of *total*
water include water from food.

## Supplements
For people without a deficiency or specific indication, most supplements show no
outcome benefit. Well-supported exceptions include folic acid in pregnancy,
vitamin B12 for those on vegan diets or with absorption issues, vitamin D where
deficiency is documented or sun exposure is limited, and iron for diagnosed
deficiency. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) accumulate and can reach toxic
levels; high-dose supplementation should be clinician-supervised. Supplements
are regulated far more loosely than drugs in most jurisdictions, and independent
testing repeatedly finds content mismatches.

## Detoxes and cleanses
The liver and kidneys perform detoxification continuously. Commercial "detox"
programmes have no credible evidence of removing toxins, and extended
juice-only or laxative-tea regimens risk electrolyte imbalance, blood-sugar
instability, and dependence on stimulant laxatives.

## Intermittent fasting
Time-restricted eating produces weight loss broadly comparable to continuous
calorie restriction of equivalent energy deficit; the mechanism is largely
reduced intake rather than timing per se. It suits some people's adherence
better than others. It is not appropriate for people who are pregnant, have a
history of eating disorders, are underweight, or take insulin or
sulfonylureas without close medical supervision.

## Limits of this section
None of this is a personalised meal plan, and it cannot account for a specific
medical condition, medication interaction, allergy, or pregnancy. Nutrition
evidence is dominated by observational studies with confounding and measurement
error, so effect sizes should be treated as approximate.
